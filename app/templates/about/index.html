Why it exists

The target is Borodinsky — dark rye, coriander, molasses-black crumb. Getting there means running two rye starters side by side, a homemade whole-rye one against a bought Soviet culture as the control, and keeping enough of a record to tell which jar and which change in the recipe actually made the difference.

Which is why nothing here is free text if it can be a number: feedings carry ratios and rise, recipes carry baker's percentages, and a bake carries the version it came from.

Finding your way round

Jars
One per starter, standing on the shelf and drawn the way it is: domed at its peak, slumped with a red corner on its tape when it is hungry. Log a feeding and the next one is due on the jar's own rhythm — its page says whether that rhythm is the jar's or the app's, and whether a feed you moved has become a fixed daily time.
The ribbon
Each jar's rise since its last feed, against the next two days, with the hours you are busy shaded and the feeds to come as rings. Jars on the same flour are also set head to head on one axis. Any chart opens to fill the window with Enlarge.
Recipes
Stored as baker's percentages against total flour, so the grams fall out of whatever batch size you ask for. Editing a recipe makes a new version rather than overwriting the old one — a loaf you baked in March still reads the way it was baked.
Plan
Lays a bake out on the clock, forwards from a start or backwards from when the bread is wanted, and flags the steps that land where you are busy or asleep. A plan that fits can be started as a bake from there.
Bakes
Started from a recipe version, with Bake this at the top of that version's page — that is what pins the loaf to the recipe as it stood. The day is drawn across the top; the stages are a running sheet you tick off, with only the step in hand open. Counter mode shows that one step large, for a phone propped by the flour. The result goes on at the end.
Hints
Short notes on the steps of a bake, a recipe's stages and a jar's page: what a first loaf got wrong, said where the next one will meet it, some with a drawing. Got it puts one away for good; settings brings them back.
Equipment
The bread maker's programs, typed in from its manual, so a bake can say which one it ran. Fill a tin on the start form scales the flour to the 2, 2.5 or 3 lb the pan takes.
Photos
On jars, feedings and bakes: the dashed square with a plus adds one, and a photo opens large to be captioned. Compare crumb on the bakes page puts two loaves side by side.

How numbers are kept

  • Weights are stored in grams and temperatures in °C — one set of numbers, no rounding drift. The units picked in settings change what you read and what you type: grams, US cups and spoons, or ounces and pounds; °F or °C.
  • Every weight field takes whichever of those you are working in. A bare number is read in the unit on display; write the unit out — 250 g, 8 oz, 1 lb 2 oz, 1 ½ cups — and that wins, whatever the setting says. A recipe typed as amounts keeps the batch it was written at, so it reads back in cups.
  • Cup readings come from a density per ingredient and are approximate by nature, so they are marked as such and nothing is ever stored that way. Grams are what reaches the database, whichever unit went in, and a recipe re-saved in cups snaps to the nearest spoon — the scale is still the authority.
  • Times are stored in UTC and shown in {{ tz_name }}.
  • Jars, recipes and bakes archive rather than delete. A notebook you can tear pages out of is not much of a record.
